Embedding Model Training: Zero to Hero

Contrastive loss, triplet loss, in-batch negatives, and hard negative mining — how meaning becomes geometry.

📘 Concept Notes

Training an embedding means learning a mapping, not hand-designing one

Earlier labs used fixed, hand-built embeddings (TF-IDF, concept maps). Real models learn text->vector via gradient descent with a special similarity-based loss, so that meaning becomes geometry: similar things end up close, different things end up far apart.

Contrastive and triplet loss both encode the same goal differently

Contrastive loss pulls positive pairs together and pushes negative pairs apart past a margin. Triplet loss makes it relative: the negative must be further from the anchor than the positive, by at least a margin. Both directly optimize for the retrieval behavior you want.

In-batch negatives make large-scale training practical

Rather than hand-labeling negative examples, treat every OTHER pair's partner in the same batch as a free negative. A batch of N positive pairs yields N x (N-1) negatives for free — this is why embedding training uses large batch sizes.

Hard negatives make training actually work

Random negatives are often already far apart, giving a weak learning signal. Hard negative mining deliberately finds confusingly-similar wrong matches, forcing the model to learn finer distinctions — and production pipelines refresh this mining as the model improves.

💡 Tips & Pitfalls

Tips

  • Larger batches give more free in-batch negatives — a real efficiency lever.
  • Refresh hard-negative mining periodically as the model improves during training.
  • Normalize embeddings to unit length so cosine similarity reduces to a simple dot product.

Common Pitfalls

  • Random negatives that are already far apart give near-zero learning signal.
  • Setting the margin too small (weak push) or too large (wasted effort).
  • Forgetting to compare against an untrained baseline when reporting accuracy.
